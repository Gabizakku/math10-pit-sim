Why the middle?

Stopping splits the race into two stints. Tyre cost for a stint of p laps is S(p) = p·t0 + k·p(p−1)/2, so a one-stop race costs T(p) = S(p) + L + S(N−p).

peak saving = k·N²/4 − L = —

Expand T(p) and the p terms collapse to (k/2)·[p(p−1) + (N−p)(N−p−1)], whose slope is 2p − N. So the best pit lap is p* = N/2 — it does not depend on t0, on k, or on the pit loss L, because that loss is paid exactly once whichever lap you choose. What L decides is whether stopping is worth it at all: against never stopping the saving is k·p·(N−p) − L, which peaks at k·N²/4 − L — the live figure is in the last line of the box above.

Paste one stint of lap times, one per line (84.53 or 1:24.53). In-laps, out-laps and traffic laps are dropped automatically before the line is fitted, so one bad lap cannot drag the slope.

Assumptions

  • Lap time on a set of tyres rises linearly with laps done: t(n) = t0 + k·n.
  • Every stop costs the same L seconds (in-lap + out-lap + stationary time together).
  • A stop resets the tyres to t0; fuel and driver effects are ignored.
  • The race is run at a constant pace — no traffic, no safety cars, no weather.
  • The default numbers are illustrative. 53 laps, an 84.5 s fresh-tyre lap, 0.035 s/lap and a 23 s pit loss were chosen so the stop-or-not decision is genuinely close. They are not a fit to published lap times — that is the next task in the plan.
  • Those are all simplifications. The result worth keeping is the shape: total time is a parabola in the pit lap, so the best stop is the middle one, and whether to stop at all is a single comparison, k·p·(N−p) against L.
